Most large economies have adopted the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting
Framework, or CARF. Approved in 2022, it makes crypto service providers
collect who their customers are and report their crypto transactions to a
tax office every year, and it lets tax offices exchange those reports
automatically with the country where the customer lives. Around 60
jurisdictions have signed up to start exchanging in 2027, with some joining
in 2028; the EU runs the same flow through its DAC8 rules.

The framework covers exchanges between crypto and ordinary money, between
one crypto asset and another, and transfers between service providers.

What it means in practice: crypto activity reaches the tax office through
the provider's report even when nothing is filed personally, so gaps
between a provider's figures and a tax return are visible on both sides.
Keeping personal records of every trade — dates, amounts, values in local
currency and fees — is the habit that keeps the numbers straight.
